II. Scientific Communications.
1. On the Systematic Position of *Balanoglossus*.
By El. Metschnikoff, Odessa.
(Conclusion.)

The sequence in which the larval organs appear demonstrates, with particular clarity, the close relationship between the *tornaria* and the larvae of echinoderms. For instance, the longitudinal ciliated band develops earlier than the anal ciliated rings; the shifting of the anal opening in echinoderm larvae is a phenomenon that occurs only after most of their typical features have been acquired. A young *tornaria* can easily be mistaken for a young *auricularia* or a starfish larva; however, it could never be confused with a young *trochosphaera* or *actinotrocha*. The resemblance to these latter two larval forms is, in fact, merely superficial; it is inconceivable that the *tornaria* represents an intermediate form between echinoderm larvae (*echinopaedia*) and the *trochosphaera*—especially since the former originate from an anal gastrula, whereas the *trochosphaera* and *actinotrocha* arise from an oral one.

The hypothesis of a close affinity between the *tornaria* and echinoderm larvae implies that the organization of the adult *Balanoglossus* can be traced back to the echinoderm type. In this respect, the structural plan presents no difficulty, as bilateral symmetry is characteristic of echinoderm larvae; the only difference is that in *Balanoglossus*, this bilateral plan persists throughout the animal's life, and a radial arrangement of organs never develops.

When examining the internal organization, we must first focus our attention on those organs that are particularly characteristic of echinoderms. In this regard, the primary role is undoubtedly played by the water-vascular system, which—as developmental history reveals—manifests in *Balanoglossus* in the form of the proboscis sac. The opening of the latter is the dorsal pore, which is homologous to the corresponding organ in echinoderms. According to observations by Spengel, the upper opening—described by Kowalevsky and Agassiz—does not exist. **[p. 154]** The primary characteristic of *Balanoglossus* is that its water-vascular sac, rather than differentiating into various sections (such as a ring or ambulacral trunks)—which are, for the most part, arranged radially—remains at an early stage of development. This represents an arrest in development, which is intrinsically linked to the absence of a radial body plan. The so-called proboscis must therefore be interpreted as a single, conical ambulacral tentacle, which can be considered analogous to similar structures found in other echinoderms, specifically holothurians. Such a rudimentary state of the water-vascular system is not a particularly striking or isolated phenomenon; it is well known that in *Synapta*, not only the blind-ending ambulacral sacs but also the longitudinal trunks are lost. Furthermore, *Synapta* resembles *Balanoglossus* in its elongated, worm-like body shape and the soft, flexible nature of its skin—a condition in which the movements of ambulacral tube feet are replaced by contractions of the body as a whole. Moreover, this single ambulacral tentacle is so robustly developed that it can pull the animal's entire body along with it. The process of filling with water—and, indeed, the overall function of the proboscis—bears a strong resemblance to the well-known phenomena associated with the ambulacral tube feet of other echinoderms.

The fact that the proboscis of the young animal bears eyes is not at all surprising, especially since the tentacles of asteroids also possess such an organ.

The peritoneal system, which plays such an important role in the morphological structure of the echinoderm body, is also found in *Balanoglossus*. Although the conditions in the adult animal have not yet been sufficiently determined in this regard, there can be no doubt that the peritoneal cavity is surrounded by membranes very similar to those found, for example, in holothurians; Kowalevsky states that the alimentary canal is firmly attached to the body wall, specifically along the two midlines, at the locations where the main vessels are situated. It should also be noted that, according to Spengel *(4)*, the "body cavity" is similar in nature to the cavity of the proboscis—which is, without question, homologous to the water-vascular system. A feature peculiar to *Balanoglossus* is the division of the peritoneal cavity into two sections (a collar segment and a trunk segment); in *Tornaria*, this is manifested as a doubling of the lateral sacs (two lateral discs and an equal number of lateral plates) and may perhaps be regarded as an initial stage in the formation of metameres. On the whole, the blood-vascular system can be traced back to the type characteristic of echinoderms; **[p. 155]** this is particularly true of the two longitudinal vessels, which are positioned similarly to those in holothurians and, likewise, emerge in connection with the peritoneal system during metamorphosis.

In general, the respiratory organs of animals are among the latest organ systems to develop, both in phylogenetic and ontogenetic terms; for this reason, one should not necessarily place great weight on their homology. We observe, for instance, that the respiratory organs of various echinoderms are not morphologically equivalent to one another. This conclusion could likewise be applied to the uniquely structured internal gills of *Balanoglossus* without undermining the animal's relationship to the echinoderms. Nevertheless, I consider it probable that the gills of *Balanoglossus* are not entirely novel organs, but rather represent late-developing water-vascular structures that have proliferated in number. Like the latter, they originate as outpocketings of the foregut, which extend toward the dorsal surface of the animal to open via paired apertures. The connection to the foregut persists throughout the animal's life—a fact evidently linked to the respiratory function of these paired structures. The multiplication of these water-vascular elements—which have evolved into gills—is no more surprising than the appearance of secondary stone canals in crinoids; the only difference is that in *Balanoglossus*, in keeping with its persistent bilateral symmetry, these secondary outpocketings of the gut are arranged metamerically. It is interesting to note that, according to Spengel’s observations, the foremost pair of gill pouches communicates with the peritoneal cavity of the collar, with the gill apertures functioning as openings for the water-vascular system.

The intestinal tract offers, in general, few points of reference for a detailed comparison with echinoderms; nevertheless, it should not be overlooked that its overall organization corresponds to that of the latter. It may also prove possible to compare the characteristic so-called hepatic appendages with the branched intestinal outgrowths of asteroids, once the histological details become better understood.

The skin reveals the most significant anatomical differences, as it lacks not only the calcareous skeleton—so ubiquitous among echinoderms—but the entire dermis as well. In contrast, the nervous system—at least insofar as we know it from Spengel’s brief report—bears an unmistakable resemblance to the corresponding organ in echinoderms. **[p. 156]** I have already made some observations above regarding the histological relationships between the muscular system of *Balanoglossus* and that of echinoderms; these suggest that the longitudinal muscle layer of the former, at least, can be traced back to the echinoderm type. The circular muscle layer, however—as described by Kowalevsky—requires further investigation before it can be interpreted in morphological terms.

While this brief overview has revealed some significant differences between *Balanoglossus* and echinoderms (notably in the formation of the skin), it has, on the whole, uncovered a very high degree of correspondence in the organization and development of both animal forms. Conversely, it would be impossible to draw a parallel between the Enteropneusta and any typical class of worms, given that *Balanoglossus* lacks the brain, excretory organs, and larval forms characteristic of worms.

Since including the Enteropneusta within the otherwise uniform group of the Echinodermata would disrupt its coherence, I propose uniting both into a common phylum: the Ambulacraria. The latter may be defined as "bilaterally organized animals with a distinct gut and vascular system, specialized water-vascular vessels, and a peritoneal system; they possess an anal gastrula and a larval form characterized primarily by a longitudinal ciliated band." Given the current state of our knowledge, we cannot determine with certainty the precise evolutionary relationships between these two major groups of the Ambulacraria. It seems more likely to me that *Balanoglossus* represents a modified animal form in which the development of a radial body plan—including the complex differentiations of the water-vascular system and the integument—has failed to occur; although it is, of course, also conceivable that *Balanoglossus* represents a more primitive form than the Echinodermata. My view is based primarily on the fact that the water-vascular rudiment in the *Tornaria* larva develops as an unpaired structure, whereas the paired blind pouches found in *Bipinnaria* and *Pluteus* larvae appear more primitive.

Accordingly, our phylum is subdivided as follows:

Phylum Ambulacraria

1. Subphylum Radiata (or Echinodermata)
2. Subphylum Bilateralia (or Enteropneusta)

The first subtype comprises animal forms with a radial body plan that develops upon a bilateral foundation, featuring a robustly developed dermis—complete with calcareous deposits—and a complex, radially organized water-vascular system. The second subtype, by contrast, is defined by a definitive bilateral-symmetrical body plan, an absence of calcareous deposits, and a poorly developed water-vascular system. **[p. 157]** Respiratory organs have not been included in the diagnosis, as they may be no more characteristic of the *Ambulacraria bilateralia* than "water-lungs" are of the echinoderm subtype.
